Twelve questions people type into Google, ChatGPT and Perplexity about client replies, scope, and getting paid. Short, structured answers with the templates you can reuse.

Replying to clients & setting scope

How do I reply when a client first messages me on Upwork?

A short, specific reply beats a long pitch. A reliable structure has four parts: acknowledge, confirm availability, ask three qualifying questions, and state the next step with a time window.

Hi [Name], thanks for reaching out — I've reviewed your brief and I'm available to take this on. Before I send a quote, could you confirm the final deliverables and formats, your target deadline, and who signs off on each stage? Once I have these, I'll send a short scope note and a fixed price within one business day. Best, [You]

The three questions worth asking before you quote: what are the final deliverables and file formats; what is the target deadline and are there hard milestones; who signs off on each stage. The time window removes the "should I follow up?" ambiguity for both sides.

What should I put in a scope of work before I quote a fixed price?

A fixed price without a written scope is the most common source of scope creep. Before naming a number, pin down five things in writing.

FieldWhat to writeWhy it protects you
DeliverablesAn explicit list, with formatsRemoves "I thought that was included"
TimelineStart date, delivery date, review dateAnchors the schedule
RevisionsHow many rounds are includedCaps unpaid rework
ExclusionsWhat is not included, quoted separatelyPrevents silent scope growth
PaymentDeposit on start, balance on deliveryAligns cash flow with milestones

Project: [title]. Deliverables: [list]. Timeline: [start] to [delivery], review on [date]. Fee: $[amount], fixed, includes [n] rounds of revisions. Not included: [list] — quoted separately. Payment: [50%] to start, [50%] on delivery. Reply "approved" and I'll get started.

Get the client to reply "approved" in writing. That single word converts a quote into an agreed starting point.

How do I tell a client their project needs more rounds than we agreed?

Point back to the agreed scope, not to the client's behavior. Separate what is included from what is extra, and price the extra before doing it.

Hi [Name], we've used the [n] revision rounds included in the agreed scope. The changes in your last note go beyond that, so here's a short add-on quote for [m] more rounds: $[amount], [x] extra days. Happy to proceed once you confirm, or we can wrap at the current version — your call.

How do I confirm who approves deliverables before I start?

Ask it in the first reply, before any work begins. One approver avoids the "my colleague wanted something else" loop.

Just to confirm before I start — who will be approving each deliverable, so I send drafts to the right person?

Put the answer into the scope note. If there are two approvers, agree on who has the final say in writing.

What's a polite way to close a project and ask for a testimonial?

Close on delivery + handover + a soft ask. Ask when the value is freshest, and make it easy by offering to draft it.

Hi [Name], everything is delivered and handed over — final files are at [link], and I've included a short handover note. It was a pleasure working with you. If you're happy with the result, would you be open to a two-line testimonial I can quote? I can draft something for you to edit so it takes a minute. Thanks again, [You]

Getting paid

My invoice is 30 days overdue — what should I actually send?

Stop guessing and use a ladder. Escalating in stages keeps the relationship intact while making the debt unambiguous in writing: friendly nudge (day 1–2), polite reminder (day 3–5), clear restatement (day 7), firm note (day 14), final notice (day 21), work-pause notice (day 30), then a final request before handoff.

Hi [Name], as invoice [number] for [amount] remains unpaid [X] days past due and I haven't had a reply, I'm pausing further work on [project] until the balance is cleared. This isn't personal — it's how I keep the business sustainable. As soon as payment lands I'm happy to pick things back up. Payment details: [method / link]. [You]

The key is to escalate on silence, not on emotion. If the client replies with a real problem, step off the ladder and resolve it. For scale: Remote's Contractor Management Report 2025 found 85% of freelancers experience late payments at least some of the time and 21% are paid late or not at all more than half the time; a Bonsai analysis of 100,000+ invoices found 29% are paid at least one day late. (Remote; Bonsai via MediaPost, secondary reporting.)

When should I stop working for a client who hasn't paid?

Pause at a clear, pre-decided trigger — not when you feel annoyed. Two conditions are worth honoring: the invoice is meaningfully overdue (many freelancers use 30 days), and you've sent several reminders with no response. Announce the pause in writing, in one thread, with the exact condition to resume.

What the pause message must contain: the invoice number, amount and days overdue; that further work is paused (state which project); the exact condition to resume ("as soon as payment lands"); payment details, so acting takes one click. Avoid framing it as a punishment.

How do I write a final payment demand that stays professional?

State facts, a deadline, and your next business step — nothing you won't actually do, and no legal threats. A final message is not the place to bluff.

Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount], due [date], is now [X] days overdue. I've contacted you on [list dates] and paused work on [date], with no payment or response. This is my final request for payment by [specific date]. If the balance isn't cleared by then, I'll [hand the matter to my accountant / stop direct follow-up]. I'd still prefer to close this amicably — one reply with a payment date is enough. [You]

Keep a single email thread. It is your clean record of reasonable, good-faith follow-up. For any actual dispute or formal recovery, consult a qualified professional in your jurisdiction — a template is not legal advice.

What should I put on my invoice so I get paid faster?

Most "late" payments trace back to a vague invoice, not a difficult client. Fix the invoice and you chase far less.

FieldWhat to writeWhy it helps
Due dateAn explicit date, not "on receipt"Sets the clock everyone can see
Payment linkA clickable method, right in the invoiceRemoves "how do I pay?"
Amount & currencyUnambiguous totalAvoids approval delays
PO / referenceTheir internal reference if requiredClears accounts-payable queues
Late termsYour agreed terms, in one neutral lineMakes later reminders factual

Put the amount and due date in the email subject line too — the accounts team can act without opening it.

Should I ask for a deposit, and how much?

For new clients, a deposit removes most collection problems before they start. A commonly used range in practice is 30–50% upfront, with the balance on delivery, written into the scope note before work begins. This is common practice, not a verified statistic.

Payment: [50%] to start, [50%] on delivery. Reply "approved" and I'll get started.

A client keeps saying "next week" — how do I respond?

Convert the vague promise into a specific, written date, and stop the open-ended loop.

Thanks for the update. To help me plan, could you confirm a specific payment date I can put in my calendar? If [specific date] doesn't work, let me know the date that does and we'll lock it in. [You]

A dated commitment is easier to follow up on than a rolling "next week". The full seven-email ladder is in the Get Paid Faster Kit.

A note on scope

These answers cover communication templates only. They contain no contract clauses, no legal advice, and no guarantee of recovery or income. Some jurisdictions have specific rules about freelance payment — for example New York City's Freelance Isn't Free Act has required written contracts and payment within 30 days for covered work since 2017, and New York State extended similar protections statewide in August 2024. That is general information, not advice; check what applies where you are and consult a qualified professional for any real dispute.

Every statistic on this page is linked to a verifiable source. Where no reliable figure exists, we label it as common practice rather than inventing a number.
