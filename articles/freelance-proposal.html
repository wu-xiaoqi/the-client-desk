How to write a freelance proposal that gets a reply

A proposal that gets a reply is about the client's problem first, your solution second, and the next step made obvious.

Published 1 November 2026 · The Client Desk · communication templates, not legal advice

Short answer

Write a proposal that opens with the client's stated problem, shows the outcome you'll deliver (not a task list), states your fee and what's included, and ends with one clear next step. A proposal that asks for a decision — not just attention — gets answered.

Lead with their problem, not your bio

The client skimmed ten proposals today. The one that gets a reply is the one that proves you understood their specific situation in the first two sentences. Save the credentials for after you've shown you get it.

Show the outcome, not the task list

  • Name the result they'll have when you're done.
  • List the work only as bullet points under it.
  • Skip the jargon that makes you sound busy.

A buyer pays for the outcome; the task list is your internal map, not their selling point.

Make the fee a non-surprise

State your fee and exactly what it includes, early enough that the client isn't hunting for it. A clear scope note attached to the proposal prevents the 'I thought that was included' conversation later.

End with one next step

'Reply yes and I'll send the agreement' beats 'Let me know your thoughts.' A proposal that asks for a decision gets one; a proposal that asks for feedback gets silence.

Reusable proposal close

Hi [Name], based on [their specific situation], here's what I'd deliver: [outcome]. The fee is $[amount], which includes [what's covered]. I'd start [timing] once we confirm. Reply yes and I'll send the agreement — or tell me if a smaller first phase makes more sense. [You]
