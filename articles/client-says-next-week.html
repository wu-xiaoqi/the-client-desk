When a client keeps saying "next week"

"Next week" isn't a date. It's a loop. The fix is to convert the promise into something both sides can put in a calendar.

Published 1 November 2026 · The Client Desk · communication templates, not legal advice

Short answer

Ask for a specific written date and stop the open-ended loop. If dates keep slipping, return to the staged escalation ladder — the escalation is about documented silence and missed commitments, not about being tough.

Convert the promise into a date

A dated commitment is easier to follow up on than a rolling "next week". Ask for one specific date, and offer an alternative if that date doesn't work. Keep the tone practical — you're planning your schedule, not making an accusation.

If dates keep slipping

One slipped date is normal. Two or three is a pattern. At that point stop negotiating dates and return to the escalation ladder: a clear restatement of the amount and days overdue, then a firm note, then a work-pause notice. The trigger is documented missed commitments, not frustration.

Keep one thread

Every reply in the same email thread builds a clean record. When you eventually need to restate the history, you can list the dates without reconstructing anything from memory.

Don't bluff

Never name a next step you won't actually take. A follow-up sequence works because it's predictable and truthful, not because it sounds severe.

Reusable template

Thanks for the update. To help me plan, could you confirm a specific payment date I can put in my calendar? If [specific date] doesn't work, let me know the date that does and we'll lock it in. [You]
