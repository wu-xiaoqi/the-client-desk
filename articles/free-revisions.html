How to handle a client who expects unlimited free revisions

Unlimited revisions is a scope problem waiting to happen. The fix is to name the boundary before, not negotiate it after.

Published 1 November 2026 · The Client Desk · communication templates, not legal advice

Short answer

State a specific revision allowance in the scope note — commonly a set number of rounds, or revisions within the agreed scope — and define what falls outside it as a change request. A boundary stated in writing before work starts reads as professional; the same boundary raised after looks like a dispute.

Name the allowance before you start

'Two rounds of revisions included' is a normal, expected term. Stated up front, it's just how the engagement works. Raised after the client has already assumed 'unlimited', it becomes a confrontation.

Separate in-scope from change

Revisions that refine the agreed deliverable are one thing; new requirements are another. Tie the boundary to the scope note so 'this is a change' is a factual reference, not an opinion.

Make extra rounds a paid option, not a fight

  • State the included rounds in the scope note.
  • Describe additional rounds as a change request at your rate.
  • Offer it as a choice, not a refusal.

This keeps the relationship cooperative while protecting your time.

If it comes up mid-project

Reference the scope note calmly: 'This is past the included rounds, so it'd be a change request — want me to quote it?' The note does the explaining; you don't have to.

Reusable revision-boundary line

Hi [Name], just to confirm the scope: the [deliverable] includes [two rounds] of revisions within the agreed spec. Anything beyond that — new requirements or a different direction — would be a change request at [rate], and I'm happy to quote it before proceeding. [You]
