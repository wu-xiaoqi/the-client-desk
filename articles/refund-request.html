How to respond when a client asks for a refund

A refund request is usually about expectations, not malice. The response should clarify what was delivered before any decision.

Published 1 November 2026 · The Client Desk · communication templates, not legal advice

Short answer

Respond to a refund request by first summarising what was delivered against the agreed scope and what remains unpaid or incomplete, then propose a proportional resolution rather than an immediate yes or no. A written record of delivered work is what makes any refund conversation fair — and defensible.

Don't refund on the first message

An immediate 'sure' can give back money for work already done. An immediate 'no' can escalate a fixable misunderstanding. The first reply should acknowledge and summarise what's been delivered, not decide.

Map the request to the scope

What did they receive versus what was agreed? If the deliverable matches the scope, the conversation is about expectations. If it doesn't, a partial or proportional resolution is fair to both. The scope note is your reference point.

Offer a proportional outcome

  • Completed work delivered → no refund, but a fix if it's within scope.
  • Partial delivery → a proportional adjustment.
  • Genuine miss → a defined remedy, agreed in writing.

A proportional offer reads as fair; an all-or-nothing stance reads as defensive.

Keep it in writing

Summarise the resolution in one message and ask the client to confirm. A refund conversation without a written summary is one both sides will remember differently later.

Reusable refund-acknowledge reply

Hi [Name], I want to get this right. Here's what's been delivered against our agreed scope: [list]. Based on that, I'd suggest [proportional resolution / a fix within scope]. If something's missing from my list, tell me and I'll address it. Happy to talk it through — [You]
