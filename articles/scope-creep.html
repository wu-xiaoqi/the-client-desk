How to handle scope creep without damaging the relationship

Scope creep rarely starts with a difficult client. It starts with a scope note that never said how many rounds were included, or what was excluded.

Published 1 November 2026 · The Client Desk · communication templates, not legal advice

Short answer

Point back to the agreed scope, not to the client's behavior. Separate what is included from what is extra, price the extra before doing it, and close with "your call" so the relationship survives the commercial line.

Point to the scope, not the person

"We've used the three rounds in the agreed scope" is a factual statement. "You keep changing your mind" is an accusation. The first one is easy to answer; the second one starts an argument.

Price the extra before doing it

Name how many additional rounds are needed, what they cost, and how many extra days they take. Wait for confirmation. Doing the work first and invoicing later converts a commercial decision into a collection problem.

Prevent it in the scope note

FieldWhat to writeWhy it protects you
DeliverablesAn explicit list, with formatsRemoves "I thought that was included"
RevisionsHow many rounds are includedCaps unpaid rework
ExclusionsWhat is not included, quoted separatelyPrevents silent scope growth
ApproversWho signs off each stageAvoids contradicting feedback

Get the word "approved"

End the scope note with "Reply 'approved' and I'll get started." That single written word converts a quote into an agreed starting point and makes any later add-on a shared decision rather than a surprise.

Reusable extra-round template

Hi [Name], we've used the [n] revision rounds included in the agreed scope. The changes in your last note go beyond that, so here's a short add-on quote for [m] more rounds: $[amount], [x] extra days. Happy to proceed once you confirm, or we can wrap at the current version — your call.
